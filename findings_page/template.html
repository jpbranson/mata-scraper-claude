Memphis Buses, Measured

Two weekdays of MATA service, read from the agency’s own bus tracker and its GTFS-Realtime feed

Thursday 24 September 2026, a full service day, and Friday 25 September to 20:20 · 351,576 bus positions from 11,030 polls of MATA’s bus tracker · 1,640 snapshots of MATA’s official feed from Friday 05:45 · 1,269 scheduled trips checked

Positions, delays and loads come from MATA’s public bus tracker, polled several times a minute. Trip IDs, departures and countdowns come from MATA’s GTFS-Realtime feed. The timetable is MATA’s published GTFS. Each graphic names the query in analysis.sql that made it.Across these two days MATA’s timetable called for 1,269 trips that can be checked, and 185 of them never ran: no bus appeared on them, in the tracker or in MATA’s own feed. That is one trip in seven, spread across the whole day and worst at 6 a.m., when 23% of trips never left.

The trips that did run left their first stop a median of 4.2 minutes behind schedule, and on most routes made the time back on the road, where the timetable has slack. They almost never bunched; at intervals of 45 to 120 minutes a late bus is simply late. A rider who reached a stop two minutes early waited a median of 4.9 minutes for the next bus, and more than a quarter of an hour 23% of the time.

MATA’s own records undercount this. Its feed marked 27 of Friday’s 101 unrun trips as canceled, and 5 trips it marked canceled did run. Its 34 rider alerts that day are dispatchers’ free text, such as “Route 1 is not running from William Hudson at 5:15a,” and some carry the wrong route tag.Some routes lost whole mornings. Route 01 (Union) lost 2 of its 45 trips on Thursday. On Friday the first of its trips to run was the 12:45, and it lost 31 of 40. On Thursday route 69 (Winchester) ran nothing before its 14:30 trip and lost 13 of 17, and route 53 (Summer) lost 19 of 31. Routes 04, 07, 13, 40 and the trolley lost nothing on either day.

Late from the first stop

MATA’s feed holds each bus at the first stop of its next trip until it leaves, which makes it a good clock for departures. Of 455 departures on Friday, 60% left on time, at most a minute early or five minutes late. Forty percent left more than five minutes late. Three left early.

Once under way, most routes recover. Following each trip from the first stop the tracker logged to the last, 15 of 24 routes finish more than two minutes earlier than they started, so their timetables carry slack. Six routes lose more time on the road: 02, 28, 01, 13, 36 and 08.

The late direction is the one leaving downtown. On 10 of the 17 bus routes that run to William Hudson Transit Center, buses leaving it are five or more minutes late at least ten points more often than buses heading toward it. Only 28 (Airport) and 40 (Stage & Lauderdale) lean the other way.

Through the day, lateness builds in the morning and peaks in the afternoon . From 15:00 to 17:59 more than a quarter of bus-polls run five minutes late. After 21:00 the problem reverses and buses run early, 27% of polls at 21:00 and 40% at 22:00, which points to evening timetables with too much time in them.

Waiting at the stop

Put lateness and missing trips together from a rider’s side. Arrive at a timepoint two minutes before the timetable says, and wait for the next bus of that route. The median wait is 4.9 minutes. On routes that lost trips it is far longer: 48 minutes on route 69, 25 on route 02, 15 on route 01.

MATA’s countdowns are accurate close in and optimistic further out . Within five minutes of a prediction, 87% of buses come within two minutes of it. Twenty to forty minutes ahead, 41% do, and 42% come a minute or more earlier than predicted. A rider who times a walk to a twenty-minute countdown will often find the bus gone.

How full the buses run

The tracker reports each bus’s load as a percentage of fifty riders, so the riders on board are that figure halved. On Thursday the network carried 4,064 rider-hours and at least 8,077 boardings , close to the 7,700 a day MATA reported for September 2023. The busiest moment on both days was 15:40, with 451 and 423 riders aboard.

The fleet was short most of the day. Against the trips the timetable had in progress, the tracker usually saw about four fewer buses on the road, and as many as thirteen fewer.

Buses are seldom crowded. Average loads run from 1 to 11 riders, and the 95th percentile tops out at 29, on route 50. Every seat of a 40-foot bus was taken on only three routes, and every reading of 96% or more was route 50 heading out to Exeter Road between 08:40 and 13:10.

On route 50, 46% of polls with twenty or more riders were five minutes late, against 16% with fewer than ten; on route 08, 58% against 26%. Boarding time may be the cause, or late buses may collect more waiting riders; the data can’t tell which. Across routes the busiest are not especially late: the correlation is 0.19.

How the numbers were checked

The tracker gives each bus’s position, delay, next stop and load every ten seconds, with no trip ID and no timestamp of its own. A few checks decide what that data can carry.

The load figure counts riders

All 351,576 load readings are even numbers, and together they cover every even value from 0 to 100. The vendor divides a rider count by 50 for every vehicle; a capacity that varied from bus to bus would produce odd percentages.

MATA’s feed also reports load, as a category. Matched to the tracker’s reading from the same report, the categories turn out to be fixed bands of that same count, and “standing room only” starts at about 11 riders, on a bus with 40 seats. They say nothing about free seats.

Speed is in metres per second

For 2,751 moving buses reported by both feeds at the same position, the tracker’s speed equals the official feed’s metres-per-second value 97% of the time. Distance covered over five-minute windows agrees, at 1.03 times speed multiplied by time.

Stuck positions are mostly buses waiting

A bus whose coordinates stop changing is either parked or carrying a tracker that has stopped reporting. How the still stretch ends tells the two apart: a parked bus drives off from the same spot, while a dead tracker reappears somewhere else. Long still stretches are layovers and holds. Dead trackers are short, and one bus, 458, accounts for nearly half of all the rows they leave behind.

Still stretches of six polls or more, by how they ended. The few not counted in a column were still under way when this snapshot ended.

They barely matter for delay, because the vendor freezes the delay along with the position. Dropping them, instead of long still stretches, moves no route’s late share by more than 1.4 points.

Trips, stops and the ends of lines

The tracker names no trip, so each bus is matched to the timetable trip due at its next stop. Checked against 60,662 reports in MATA’s feed, the match agrees 99.8% of the times it names a trip, and it names none 2.6% of the time, mostly between trips. The log of when buses serve stops catches 84% of the stops on a trip that ran. At the end of a line the tracker still shows the finished trip’s destination as the bus leaves, so departures there are timed from MATA’s feed instead.

Not yet answerable

Weekday against weekend, weather and events need weeks of data. The effect of detours needs the poller’s new detour log to run for weeks. The minute window MATA uses for its own on-time figures isn’t published; this page uses the usual one, at most one minute early and five minutes late.
